# Gift story reasoning

## Build from visual affordances

Identify what can move, transform, respond, reveal space, or carry emotion. Select actions already supported by the reference: wheels rotate, wings unfold, lanterns rise, flowers bloom, sand falls, instruments are played, water splashes, leaves drift, animals walk or turn, and characters exchange restrained gestures. Use light propagation only when the source and activation logic are supplied or clearly inherent.

Do not invent an unrelated journey merely to add spectacle. Preserve the image's subject hierarchy and make the reference content the cause or destination of the story. The actual subject should normally initiate the action; do not place an invented messenger or magical guide in front of it.

## Creative expansion beyond the frame

A reference image defines anchors, not the complete boundary of the video. For L2VA and reference-based gifts, confidently infer unseen but compatible content when it improves the story or shot contrast:

- an adjacent room, street, platform, shoreline, forest path, deck, tunnel, underwater zone, rooftop, or backstage area;
- an earlier object state, preparation action, approach, arrival, release, opening, launch, or discovery;
- a supporting prop, vehicle, door, container, path, weather event, or mechanical process consistent with the world;
- a different time or lighting phase that visibly transitions into the reference state.

The invented content must pass four checks:

1. **World fit:** same design language, materials, period, palette family, and physical rules.
2. **Causal fit:** Shot 1 creates a question or action that Shot 2 clearly answers.
3. **Value fit:** scale and object symbolism remain inside the 1000-3000 range unless supplied by the user.
4. **Anchor recovery:** the climax and later shots preserve every important reference identity, structure, layout, and composition language. Require exact final-frame recovery only for an explicitly requested L2VA endpoint.

Do not keep both shots trapped inside the visible reference composition. Do not merely place a foreground object over the same view. Prefer a genuinely new space or event when the user explicitly requests multiple shots.

## Emotional temperature and action density

Classify the reference before writing actions:

- **Quiet or intimate:** contemplation, moonlight, waiting, companionship, listening, gentle romance. Use one deliberate subject action per shot at most. Keep body motion restrained, but let the camera perform one confident spatial reveal at normal speed before easing during the final cover; use breathing, fabric, foliage, water, or practical-light motion for residual life.
- **Lyrical or playful:** discovery, flirtation, small animals, flowers, travel, light celebration. Use one clear action plus one response per shot, with moderate camera energy.
- **Kinetic or performative:** vehicles, flight, chase, dance, music, sports, machinery. Allow two or three linked actions per shot when their mechanics remain readable.

Shot count controls editorial viewpoints, not mandatory body-action count. A quiet two-shot gift may use an intimate detail followed by a wide emotional reveal without making the character stand, walk, turn, sit, and pose inside five seconds. Preserve the reference's emotional pace even when the camera changes boldly.

## Pose formation and editorial ellipsis

Before animating a final seated, mounted, reclining, embraced, or balanced pose, ask whether entering that pose is itself the story:

- If the posture change is simple, safe, visually central, and has enough time, show its mechanics in positive order.
- If it is complex, incidental, occluded, or likely to look rushed, cut across elapsed time. Start the later shot with the subject already stably posed, then animate the emotional payoff and natural residual motion.
- Preserve identity, costume, prop ownership, geography, and story causality across the time jump, but do not preserve the exact body-action phase when doing so forces implausible movement.

A seamless visual bridge may still conceal an editorial time jump. Seamless means perceptually smooth, not physically continuous in every limb. Do not squeeze climbing onto a sill, sitting on the ground, boarding a boat, mounting a swing, or arranging a long dress into less than about one second unless the action is explicitly requested and fully staged.

## Environmental performance layer

Choose one or two existing environmental actors per shot and give them specific, low-cost behavior tied to the scene:

- animals notice, approach, change perch, cross the foreground, or settle;
- curtains, hair, ribbons, leaves, wheat, snow, water, and flower stems respond to wind or subject motion;
- practical lights, reflections, instruments, wheels, doors, or mechanisms respond to a visible cause;
- foreground objects provide parallax or framing without becoming arbitrary transition devices.

Environmental motion can carry a quiet shot so the character does not need extra gestures. Keep it subordinate to the main subject, physically motivated, and consistent with the reference. Do not replace natural behavior with generic particles, light sweeps, or compulsory magical responses.

## Narrative archetypes

Choose one primary archetype unless the user supplies another structure.

### Summoning and discovery

A supplied or visually justified light, sound, reflection, breeze, or messenger attracts the subject toward a destination. Best for animals, characters, paths, doors, gardens, and hidden environments. Do not choose this archetype merely because the input lacks a plot.

### Growth and formation

A seed, sapling, crystal, ornament, or partial structure grows or assembles through visible stages. Describe directional extension, thickening, branching, attachment, and settling; never use uniform scale-up as a substitute.

### Awakening and illumination

A core motif explicitly present in the reference or request activates a larger object or environment through a readable propagation path. Illuminate local detail first, then structure, then surroundings. Do not invent an activation beam for an ordinary realistic scene, and do not switch the entire frame on simultaneously.

### Passage and reveal

The camera or subject travels through a tunnel, slide, forest, cloud, water, doorway, or foreground occluder into a wider scene. Preserve direction and momentum across the reveal.

### Encounter and healing

One subject approaches, observes, makes gentle contact, and receives a response. Keep cause-and-effect order explicit and body contact physically safe.

### Chase and ride

A leaf, vehicle, train, light, or small creature leads fast camera motion. Maintain a unique front, route, speed logic, and safe camera distance.

### Material gathering and assembly

Petals, dust, light threads, water, or crystals converge to form one referenced object in structural order. Avoid duplicate components, melting, and explosive concealment.

### Festival and performance

A performer, lantern, firework, stage prop, or festive object creates a rhythmic sequence and activates environmental elements. Preserve readable performance mechanics and existing visible text only.

### Giving and response

A character or object offers, catches, holds, releases, or transforms a gift. Focus on hands, contact, delayed material response, and a clear emotional payoff.

### Small ritual and blessing

A restrained light, flower, emblem, crown, or charm completes a ceremonial state around a central subject. Keep the scale appropriate unless explicitly supplied otherwise.

## Value curve

Use this functional curve rather than a fixed plot template:

1. **Hook:** show a readable motion, detail, contact, mechanical action, or justified light cue within about 0.5 seconds.
2. **Cause:** make the subject notice, receive, release, activate, follow, or begin moving.
3. **Escalation:** increase speed, space, structure, illumination, or emotional proximity.
4. **Climax:** form one unmistakable gift moment centered on the referenced subject.
5. **Dynamic cover:** reduce camera amplitude while retaining appropriate natural motion.

For four seconds, combine cause and escalation. For six seconds, allow two clear escalation stages. Do not fill extra time with unrelated effects.

## Time budgeting

- Reserve enough time for contact, landing, transformation, or stopping to be visible rather than implied.
- Prefer one major state change per roughly 0.5-0.8 seconds.
- Use the final 0.6-0.8 seconds for the moving cover by default in a five-second multi-shot gift. Extend it only when the user explicitly prioritizes a long hold.
- When a user supplies dense timing, preserve hard-priority actions and remove or compress inferred decoration first.

## Two-shot story architecture

Design each shot with a different job:

- **Shot 1 — premise or kinetic hook:** establish an unseen cause, approach, preparation, obstacle, or intimate detail.
- **Shot 2 — reveal and payoff:** emerge through a seamless bridge into a clearly different viewpoint or space, reveal the referenced subject and environment, complete the main action, and ease into the dynamic cover.

Good contrast pairs include macro object to aerial environment, interior preparation to exterior result, underwater travel to island panorama, moving vehicle close-up to destination-wide reveal, intimate gesture to landscape-scale cover, and backstage mechanics to frontal performance. Avoid close-follow to slightly wider follow in the same direction and same location.

## Action-capacity budget

Assign each shot one primary action chain and at most one environmental response. For three shots, use this default division: Shot 1 supplies one hook or preparation action; Shot 2 supplies one spatial advance or state change; Shot 3 supplies one climax action and converges to the moving cover. Remove secondary gestures before compressing the primary chain. Reject bundles such as walking several steps, turning, taking a prop, performing, and looking up inside one short shot.

## Reference-anchor priority

Rank visual commitments internally before allocating actions:

1. **Level 1:** character identity, subject count, core object, and final pose. These never yield to decorative action.
2. **Level 2:** costume construction, prop orientation, architectural layout, and major color relationships. Preserve unless the user explicitly changes them.
3. **Level 3:** exact petal or particle count, distant small objects, and local ornament. Simplify these first when duration, action capacity, or model stability is limited.

## Climax design

The climax should be identifiable at thumbnail size. Center the primary subject, clarify silhouette, preserve face or emblem readability, and let supporting elements frame rather than cover it. Use restrained synchronized responses such as one blossom wave, one wing expansion, one mechanical start, one physical arrival, one justified illumination path, or one camera settle. A climax does not require a visual effect.

Do not freeze. Retain low-amplitude motion appropriate to the scene: breathing, feather or wing tremble, fabric settling, wheel rotation, water ripples, falling petals, drifting snow, moving reflection, foliage sway, light flow, or ongoing performance.
