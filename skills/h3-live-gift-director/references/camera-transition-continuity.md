# Camera, transition, and continuity grammar

## Camera quality baseline

Every price tier requires a precise camera path with a readable subject, direction, amplitude, speed, and easing. Use physical camera translation for spatial reveals rather than stationary digital zoom. Unless the reference is intentionally static, give the main reveal one medium-to-large change in scale or camera position and let it accelerate clearly before easing near the final cover. Treat subject emotion and camera energy as separate decisions: a quiet character may remain still while the camera cranes through foliage, travels from water level to overhead, or arcs from profile to a wide environment. Avoid uncontrolled rotation, repeated 360-degree rolls, camera inversion, subject penetration, collisions, and directionless motion.

## Useful camera patterns

- **Approach-pass-return:** track from behind, pass beside or below the subject, then settle into a stable follow position.
- **Low chase to reveal:** match a moving subject at low height, then rise and pull out while the subject maintains ground speed.
- **Orbit and recenter:** perform a limited arc around the subject, then correct toward the final frontal or three-quarter composition.
- **POV to exterior:** hide the viewpoint conversion under complete hand, fabric, water, wing, or light occlusion.
- **Detail to panorama:** begin on one functional detail, follow its motion, then use parallax to reveal the environment.
- **Keyframe pass-through:** reach a reference-supported intermediate angle briefly and continue moving rather than freezing.

## Functional shot assignment

Give every shot one camera job before designing its movement. Typical jobs are: prove a hand or instrument action, establish subject identity, reveal geography, show a mechanical cause, follow travel, or deliver the final cover. Do not ask one short shot to perform all jobs at once.

When the user or story requires a close-up, lock it operationally:

1. state the exact included anatomy or mechanism, such as fingertips, strings, bridge and lower sleeve;
2. state the excluded content, such as face, full torso, full instrument or surrounding stage outside frame;
3. state the minimum interval during which the crop stays locked;
4. state the focus target and visible mechanical response;
5. reveal the wider subject only after the detail action is complete.

Example: `For the first 0.7 seconds, the frame contains only the right hand, plectrum, pipa strings, bridge and embroidered cuff; the performer's face and full body remain outside frame. Focus stays on fingertip contact and string vibration.` This prevents H3 from treating "close-up" as a mild push-in on a medium shot.

## In-shot camera grammar

Use a readable two-stage path inside each shot:

- **acquire -> follow/reveal:** begin on the functional detail, lock its action, then translate or arc to reveal the subject or space;
- **establish reset -> recenter/settle:** begin the incoming shot at its clearly new scale and axis, then perform one principal move and one small correction toward the cover.

Prefer one main motion plus one correction per shot. For quiet scenes, reduce subject-action density; retain a confident camera path and ease only as the cover arrives. For kinetic scenes, camera energy may rise further, but the subject's mechanics must remain legible. Avoid both static push-only staging and a chain of unrelated pans, rolls, orbits, cranes, and zooms.

## Transition priority

First decide whether the user asked for a hard cut, seamless multi-shot structure, or a continuous take. For an ordinary two-shot or three-shot live gift, default to a perceptually seamless transition that still creates a new shot with a new scale, axis, space, or information. Use a visible hard cut only when the user requests it or when no causal bridge can exist. A seamless transition is not a continuous take: the incoming shot still resets the viewpoint after the bridge.

## Seamless-but-distinct protocol

Use this order for every requested multi-shot seamless transition:

1. **Design the shot boundary first.** Define Shot 1's outgoing composition and Shot 2's incoming composition before selecting any bridge, occluder, or match action.
2. **Make the reset substantial.** Change at least two contrast dimensions and preferably three: scale, height or axis, primary information, action phase, or spatial scope.
3. **Choose the bridge second.** Select an in-world material already produced by the action or environment: water from a bow wave, snow from a throw, wheat bent by a vehicle, fabric from a gesture, foliage beside a path, or an architectural surface crossed by the camera.
4. **Specify physical coverage when coverage is the chosen family.** State the bridge's source, force, direction, depth layers, and the exact instant when it covers all four edges. Change viewpoint only at complete coverage.
5. **Declare the new shot immediately without hard-cut language.** After the timestamp, state the bridge and the new scale, height, side, or location in the first clause, for example: `[Shot 2] At 00:01.200, as the foreground leaves clear, a high rear wide view reveals...`.
6. **Carry little, reset much.** Preserve one or two necessary variables, such as vehicle travel direction and the water's downward drainage, while deliberately resetting camera position, lens scale, angular motion, or altitude.
7. **Test the three frames.** The frame before coverage and the frame after clearing must be recognizably different shots, while the fully covered middle frame makes the change feel smooth.

This protocol creates a seamless editorial transition: the viewer feels one physical flow but clearly reads two separately staged shots.

## Transition-family selection

Choose the bridge that the action and setting can physically supply. Do not default every video to a sleeve, tree trunk, column, or curtain covering the lens.

- **Entity occlusion:** a curtain, column, tree trunk, vehicle body, or sail crosses the camera because camera or subject travel naturally places it there.
- **Action match:** a hand raise, foot landing, turn, instrument stroke, wingbeat, or release continues at the same action phase from a new scale or axis.
- **Shape match:** an existing circular lantern may align with a moon, or a wheel with a Ferris wheel, when the two objects already belong to the story; the match does not create a new effect or causal link.
- **Direction match:** a subject exits left-to-right and enters a different space continuing left-to-right, while camera height, scale, or axis changes clearly.
- **Depth-of-field cover:** a real foreground flower, leaf, branch, or architectural detail moves out of focus across the full view before the new viewpoint emerges.
- **Traversal:** the camera passes through a real doorway, window, arch, tunnel mouth, or water surface that connects or plausibly separates the spaces.
- **Natural material:** sourced snow, water spray, falling leaves, dust, or smoke fills the view because of an explicit throw, impact, wake, wind gust, or practical source.

For a three-shot video, use two different transition families. Do not repeat the same full-coverage family at both boundaries unless the user explicitly asks for a recurring motif.

### Reliable contrast-and-bridge pairs

- control, cabin, room, or cockpit macro -> exterior environmental wide, bridged by a window wipe, door surface, water, fabric, or another action-caused full occlusion;
- wheel, hoof, hand, instrument, or mechanical detail -> high, rear, overhead, or frontal wide reveal, bridged by nearby vegetation, clothing, snow, spray, or a foreground structural surface;
- shoreline or water-level action -> aerial or elevated landscape reveal, bridged by a wave, foam, bubbles, or a passing solid foreground object;
- intimate character gesture -> distant ensemble or environment cover, bridged by sleeves, hair, petals, snow, a door frame, or a match-on-action cut;
- moving subject close-up -> destination-wide reveal, bridged by a tunnel edge, tree trunk, passing vehicle body, tall plants, or terrain.

Do not force an occluder when none is physically available. A motivated match cut or clean editorial cut is better than an arbitrary object crossing the screen.

### Multi-shot contrast contract

Every later shot must change at least two of these dimensions, and preferably three:

1. shot scale by at least one clear step, such as macro to wide or close to aerial wide;
2. camera side, height, or axis, such as frontal low angle to rear high angle;
3. primary visual information, such as traveler to destination, exterior to interior, approach to revealed result;
4. subject state or action phase, such as closed to open, moving to arrived, alone to assembled;
5. spatial scope, such as one detail to the complete environment.

For a two-shot request, aim for a bold contrast rather than the minimum pass and connect it seamlessly by default. When the reference is a final cover image, let Shot 1 occupy an inferred off-frame space or earlier event and let Shot 2 reveal the reference world from a new axis. H3 can generate unseen content; use that capacity deliberately instead of orbiting near the reference composition in both shots.

Recommended contrast ladders:

- macro or interior close-up -> exterior wide or aerial reveal;
- underwater or below-surface tracking -> high island panorama;
- frontal moving subject -> rear or overhead destination view;
- dark preparation space -> bright completed environment;
- isolated subject detail -> full ensemble or facility result;
- platform, backstage, cabin, tunnel, or shop interior -> open landscape or public stage.

Do not place Shot 2 in nearly the same framing, distance, side, and space as Shot 1. Do not preserve all camera direction, speed, and angular momentum across an explicit cut. Preserve only the subject continuity variables needed for coherence, while deliberately changing the editorial viewpoint.

Write the seamless boundary plainly: `[Shot 2] At 00:02.300, as the action-matched foot lands, a high wide rear view reveals...` State the new framing, bridge, and new information in the opening clause. Do not write `the camera cuts to` unless a visible hard cut is intended.

### Transition choice

- Use an action, shape, or direction match when it connects two clearly different compositions without needing full coverage.
- Use an occlusion transition only when it enables a genuine viewpoint or spatial jump. If the outgoing and incoming views are similar, redesign the shot contrast rather than adding a decorative wipe.
- Use a direct motivated hard cut only when the user asks for one or when a major time/location jump has no physically coherent bridge.
- Use a hidden perspective conversion only for a continuous-take request.

When an occlusion cut is genuinely useful, prefer in-world occluders:

- petals, leaves, ribbons, tails, wings, fabric;
- water splashes, foam, bubbles, ripples, snow;
- hands, sleeves, vehicle bodies, tree trunks, architecture;
- firework trails, energy ribbons, stardust, illuminated smoke used sparingly.

When no causal seamless bridge exists, use a concise direct editorial change only as a last resort. Keep transition failure modes in the internal review checklist rather than naming them in the generated H3 prompt.

### Failure patterns to reject

- **Decorative wipe without editorial change:** an object crosses the screen, but Shot 2 resumes at nearly the same distance, side and camera path.
- **Pseudo-one-take continuity:** both shots preserve the same orbit, angular velocity, height and subject distance, so H3 renders one uninterrupted move.
- **Transition-first design:** a convenient petal, light streak or foreground object is chosen before the incoming shot is designed, leaving no meaningful reveal behind it.
- **Uncaused occluder:** water, snow, petals, smoke or fabric fills the frame without a visible physical source.
- **Partial-cover cut:** the edit happens while corners or subject fragments remain visible, producing a hard jump.
- **Occluder-led Shot 2 opening:** the prompt spends the opening of Shot 2 continuing the wipe and delays stating the new wide, aerial, exterior or reverse viewpoint.
- **Excessive continuity variables:** camera direction, speed, roll, orbit and subject position all continue unchanged. Preserve only what the story needs.

## Full-occlusion rule

A hidden cut may occur only after the occluder covers the complete frame. Describe the incoming surface, texture, direction, and the exact moment of full coverage. The outgoing shot must reveal a materially different viewpoint, scale, space, or state. If the shot merely continues from the same angle behind the same moving object, the cut has no editorial value and should be redesigned.

## Continuity contract

Across every cut, preserve only the applicable continuity variables; do not preserve the camera so strongly that the cut disappears:

1. subject travel direction and speed when the same movement continues;
2. action phase or directional screen logic when needed for a match cut;
3. gait, wheel, wingbeat, strum, jump, or gesture phase;
4. particle, petal, water, snow, or smoke direction when physically continuous;
5. subject identity, count, front/back orientation, and object order;
6. spatial geography and destination.

Allow and require a clear camera reset in scale, height, axis, or side for an explicit new shot.

Examples:

- A raised foreleg before a tree-trunk wipe lands as the first stride after the cut.
- A clockwise tail wipe clears clockwise from the opposite side after the cut.
- A train remains locomotive-first, with the same speed and carriage order.
- A snowflake filling a globe becomes the same moving snowflake in the full-scale world.

## Continuous-take rule

When the user requests one continuous shot, use only `[Shot 1]`. Occlusion may hide a perspective or scale conversion only if the resulting path remains geographically and visually plausible; do not describe an actual cut. Maintain an unbroken camera trajectory before, during, and after the occlusion.

## Final camera settle

Complete the main acceleration before the cover interval. During the final roughly 0.6-0.8 seconds of a five-second multi-shot gift, keep a controlled residual drift or continuing directional move while the reference composition remains readable. Hold stable exposure and full color through the last frame. Keep unwanted ending modes in the internal review checklist rather than naming them in the generated prompt.
